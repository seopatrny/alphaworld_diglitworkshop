Phase 1 0–15 min

The question on the wall

When students arrive, one question is already written on the wall: "What does the internet know about you?" No explanation. Let it sit.

"Think of one specific moment when technology gave you access to something that changed how you saw yourself or the world. A real moment. Not a general answer. Ninety seconds."

Everyone speaks. The guide reflects the thread back: "For most of you, technology wasn't a tool — it was a door."

Then the pivot: "The girls we're designing for have a different relationship with that door. Today we figure out where they actually are."

Guide note

Listen for what's underneath — not the technology they mention, but the access it gave them. That's the thread. Reflect it back before pivoting. Don't rush the silence after the prompt.

Your notes — Phase 1
Phase 2 15–35 min

What does the internet know about them?

Pairs of Alpha students use Claude + web search to find specific, surprising facts. Three research questions on the table:

"What does the internet currently say about a community like Legacy Girls School in rural Kenya? What's represented? What's missing?"
"What do girls in rural Kenya actually use mobile technology for now — and what do they say they want to use it for?"
"What have other community-led digital literacy programs in East Africa built? What worked — and what was designed for the wrong person?"

Each pair shares their most important finding. The room shifts. What surfaces every time: the internet knows almost nothing about this community, and what it does know was written by outsiders. The question on the wall stops being abstract.

Guide note

Capture findings on the whiteboard in real time. The gap between "what exists" and "what's missing" becomes the design brief for Phase 3. Don't interpret — let the cohort sit with what they found.

Your notes — Phase 2
Phase 3 35–65 min

Live the lesson, then build it

The cohort does the work themselves before they design it for anyone else. Two moves in 30 minutes.

First 10 min — your own footprint

Each Alpha student uses AI to investigate their own digital footprint. What's out there? What surprised you? What did you not know was searchable? Note the moment of surprise — that's the moment Lesson 1 has to engineer for Legacy students.

Next 20 min — build Lesson 1

With the five digital literacy pillars on the table and the footprint exercise fresh, the cohort co-designs the lesson that walks Legacy students through the same discovery — adapted for their context, their tools, their lives.

The Hook
The question that opens it. Personal, not academic.
The Discovery
What Legacy students investigate in the first 20 minutes.
The Skill
One concrete digital capability they walk away with.
The Output
What they produce that didn't exist before the session.
The Bridge
The question that pulls Legacy students into Lesson 2.
Guide note

Don't impose solutions. When the group stalls: "Who is this actually for? What assumption are you making? What would Mama Jane think of this?" Don't fill silences too fast.

Test to pass — keep in mind

What you build today is a hypothesis. In weeks 7–9, you'll teach this lesson to real Legacy students, measure whether they learned, and revise until it works without you. Design with that standard in mind.

Your notes — Phase 3
Phase 4 65–80 min

Pressure test

One Alpha student teaches the lesson. The others play Legacy girls encountering it cold — curious, bright, no prior context about digital literacy.

This hits harder now because the cohort just lived through their own version of it.

Five minutes, then stop:

"What landed? What confused? What did the lesson assume you knew that you didn't? What moment did the room actually lean in?"

Always reveals one or two changes that make the lesson significantly stronger. This is the dress rehearsal for something they will do for real.

Your notes — Phase 4
Phase 5 80–90 min

Name it and own it

Three moves.

Name the curriculum. Not a placeholder. A name that reflects whose question it centers — and that Legacy students can claim ownership of when they meet it.

Own a lesson. Each Alpha student takes ownership of at least one of the 9 lessons in the arc. Not designing them yet — putting names on chairs so everyone knows what they're building.

Commit out loud.

"By the time we leave Kenya, the thing I'm most committed to making sure exists is..."

Every answer goes on the wall. It stays there for nine weeks.
