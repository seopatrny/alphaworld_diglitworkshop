The Brief

Build it. Teach it. Prove it works. Leave it behind.

Digital literacy was identified as a priority by the partner school leadership and the Legacy Plus team — a partner embedded in this community for over 20 years. The Alpha cohort's job is not to determine what the community needs. It's to design curriculum that delivers on a need that already exists, in a way Kenyan teachers can sustain long after the cohort leaves.

On Day 1, the cohort produces the first draft of that curriculum. Draft is the operative word. What gets built in 90 minutes is a hypothesis — a starting point the cohort will pressure-test against real community input in the first weeks of the rotation before anything is taught.

The curriculum evolves. The standard doesn't: it runs without us.

By week 9, a Kenyan student will have authored something about her community that the internet didn't know before. By the time the Alpha cohort flies home, the curriculum that got her there can be picked up and run by a Kenyan teacher who wasn't in the room.

What we're teaching

Digital literacy — defined

Digital literacy is the ability to find, evaluate, use, and create information using digital technologies — effectively and responsibly. It's not just knowing how to use a device. It's being a capable, critical, and responsible participant in digital environments.

The curriculum covers five pillars. The 9-week arc dedicates time to each one.

01
Finding & evaluating
Search effectively, assess source credibility, spot misinformation and bias
02
Using tools & platforms
Become functional with software, apps, and services relevant to your context
03
Creating content
Produce documents, media, and communications using digital tools
04
Safety & privacy
Protect personal data, recognize scams, manage access and privacy settings
05
Critical thinking about tech
Understand how algorithms, feeds, and AI shape what you see and experience
The Transformation

What an Alpha student becomes

The Alpha student who finishes this workshop can walk into a room, research a community they don't belong to, design curriculum for it, deliver it live, measure whether it actually taught somebody something, and revise it until it does. That's a skill most adults never acquire. They'll have it at sixteen.

The Standard
Test to pass — the curriculum runs without us
Weeks 7–9

Each Alpha student must lead a live session with Kenyan students, collect measurable learning data, and submit a documented revision before passing.

01
Lead a live session
Facilitate the assigned lesson with actual Kenyan students. Full class period. Real audience. No rehearsal.
Measure
Session completed and observed by another cohort member with written feedback against a shared rubric.
02
Prove they learned
Collect evidence learning happened — not vibes, not completion. Exit check, produced artifact, verbal assessment, before/after demo.
Measure
At least 70% of Kenyan students demonstrate the target skill or explain the core concept in their own words.
03
Revise for someone who wasn't there
Submit the actual updated lesson — not "I would change X" — handoff-ready to a Kenyan teacher who wasn't in the room.
Measure
Revision includes what changed, why, and evidence from the live session. Teacher-ready without an Alpha student present.

Pass threshold: All three. A student who leads but doesn't measure, or measures but doesn't revise, has not passed. The test closes when the curriculum can be run by someone who never met us.

AI Use

The medium is the message

The workshop models the digital literacy it teaches. Alpha students research, evaluate sources, and co-create using the same tools they'll eventually hand to Kenyan students.

Live community research
Claude + web search investigates real gaps in how the partner community appears online — during Day 1, in real time.
Curriculum stress-testing
AI pressure-tests lesson structure before live sessions — flagging assumptions, surfacing gaps, checking cultural fit.
Teaching to evaluate AI
Later weeks teach Kenyan students to evaluate what they find online — including AI outputs themselves.
Co-authoring
Weeks 5–9, Kenyan students use AI as a writing partner to put their community's knowledge online on their own terms.
